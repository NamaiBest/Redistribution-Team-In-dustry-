Demand Censoring, Discount Response, and Same-Day Store Redistribution in Perishable Retail

Course Project Summary Report  |  Business Analytics & Industry 4.0  |  IIT Madras B.S. Degree Programme

1. Problem Statement

Perishable fresh-retail operations face pronounced intraday shopping waves, promotional discount surges, and calendar or weather shifts, while frequent daytime stockouts censor observed sales below true demand. Relying on raw point-of-sale records understates demand for fast-moving items and misreads promotional lift. Using FreshRetailNet-50K ([1]), this study measures how stockouts distort observed sales, estimates discount response and calendar/weather effects, compares management groups and category co-occurrence descriptively, and designs a same-day store-to-store redistribution model that recovers part of the demand lost to stockouts.

2. Motivation for Problem Selection

Fresh produce operates under tight shelf-life windows: over-ordering causes spoilage and under-ordering causes immediate lost sales, often in the same city on the same day. Point-of-sale logs record nothing once a shelf empties, so demand is hidden exactly when it is highest. Combining Business Analytics (regression, elasticity, chi-square testing) with Industry 4.0 tools (association rules, inventory principles, and Linear Programming) lets us move from describing stockouts to a concrete redistribution decision.

3. Research Questions

  • RQ1 (Intraday Dynamics & Censoring): How do hourly sales and stockout rates evolve across the trading day, and how far do stockouts pull observed sales below in-stock sales?
  • RQ2 (Discounts & Promotions): How do discount depth and campaign flags relate to daily sales, and how responsive is demand to the discount multiplier?
  • RQ3 (Calendar, Weather & Management Groups): How do weekends, holidays and weather shift demand, and how do management groups differ in sales and stockout exposure?
  • RQ4 (Co-occurrence & Redistribution): Which categories co-occur across store-days and which are most stockout-exposed, and how much lost demand can a linear program recover by moving stock between stores?

4. Hypotheses, Objectives, and Constraints

Hypotheses: (H1) Sales peak twice a day while stockouts accumulate toward closing; (H2) demand is elastic to the discount multiplier (ε < −1), with discount depth rather than campaign tags driving lift; (H3) weekends and holidays raise demand and management groups differ in stockout exposure; (H4) top categories co-occur beyond chance, and unsold stock at some stores can recover demand lost at others.

Objectives: Decompose hourly sales and stockout arrays; estimate a constant-elasticity model with store-product clustered standard errors; test calendar/weather effects via Multiple Linear Regression (MLR); profile management groups; mine store-day association rules; and plan same-day transfers between stores with a Linear Program built on validated lost-demand estimates.

Constraints & Scope: sale_amount is normalised, not units or revenue; discount is a relative price multiplier (1.00 = full price); stock on hand, spoilage, costs and store locations are not recorded; and basket receipts are unobserved, so co-occurrence is measured at store-day level. Findings are observational, not causal.

5. Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA)

All results use the full training set: 4,500,000 store-product-days (18 cities, 898 stores, 865 products, 28 Mar – 25 Jun 2024), plus the 7-day evaluation week for validation. Daily sales are right-skewed (mean 0.999, median 0.70), and products average 3.18 stockout hours out of 16 daytime hours (06:00–22:00). Campaign days (activity_flag = 1) are 37.8% of rows. Discount tiers: full price 48.5%, light (0.86–0.99) 24.8%, moderate (0.71–0.85) 19.7%, deep (≤ 0.70) 7.1%. 97.8% of campaign days carry a discount (mean multiplier 0.80 vs 0.98 otherwise), so campaign and discount effects must be separated in RQ2.

6. Methodology

Techniques follow the Business Analytics and Industry 4.0 syllabus ([2], [3]):

  1. RQ1: Averaged the 24-hour hours_sale and hours_stock_status arrays to compare overall hourly sales with in-stock hourly sales, and tracked sales across stockout-duration buckets within baseline-demand quartiles (Q1–Q4).
  2. RQ2: Log-log model ln(sale) = β0 + ε·ln(discount) + β2·activity_flag + β3·stockout_hours on days with positive sales, with standard errors clustered by store-product.
  3. RQ3: χ² test (Cramér's V) of holiday vs stockout incidence; MLR of daily sales on weekend, holiday, precipitation, temperature, humidity and wind, excluding stockout hours because they are an operational outcome, with store-product clustered errors; and a descriptive comparison of management groups.
  4. RQ4 co-occurrence: Top eight categories by total sales; support, confidence and lift over all 80,820 store-days, including days without sales in a category; stockout exposure per category.
  5. RQ4 redistribution: An hourly demand profile per store-product, learned from in-stock hours only, estimates lost demand in stockout hours; it was validated by hiding hours on fully stocked days of the held-out week. Per city, product and day, a Linear Program (scipy.optimize.linprog) chooses units xij moved from store i to j to maximise Σ(pj − c)·xij (receiver price multiplier minus handling cost 0.15), subject to sent ≤ sender's unsold stock and received ≤ receiver's lost demand after arrival. Only stores with no stockout that day can send.

7. Results

RQ1: Hourly sales peak at 09:00 (0.107) with a second wave at 15:00–16:00 (0.081), while the stockout rate climbs from 6% at 06:00 to 41.9% at 22:00 (daytime average 19.9%). In-stock hourly sales exceed overall hourly sales by up to 53% (20:00). This gap shows how far stockouts pull observed sales down; it is not by itself a measure of lost demand, which RQ4 estimates and validates. In the highest-demand quartile, mean daily sales fall from 2.96 (1–3h stockout) to 2.36 (4–8h), 1.53 (9–15h) and 0.08 (full-day stockout).

RQ2: Deep discounts (≤ 0.70) coincide with 2.48× higher mean daily sales than full price (2.06 vs 0.83). The discount elasticity is ε = −1.19 (95% CI −1.23 to −1.16, clustered by store-product): a 1% lower price multiplier is associated with ~1.2% higher sales. Holding discount fixed, campaign days show 8.9% lower sales, so price cuts, not campaign tags, carry the lift. Each stockout hour is associated with 2% lower sales. Promotions and stockouts may affect each other, so these are associations, not causal effects.

RQ3: Holidays and stockouts are statistically related (χ² = 27.2, p < 0.001) but negligibly so (V = 0.003). Weekends (+0.149, t = 109) and holidays (+0.123, t = 57) raise daily sales; temperature (+0.023 per unit) and humidity raise them slightly; wind lowers them (−0.028). Calendar and weather explain only 1.2% of variation (R² = 0.012): demand is driven mostly by store and product. Management group 6 holds 53% of observations and the highest mean sales (1.15); group 2 has the highest stockout incidence (54% of days); groups 1 and 4 have the lowest sales (0.54, 0.57).

RQ4 (co-occurrence): Seven of the top eight categories appear on 90–98% of store-days, so lifts are close to 1 (highest 1.085, Categories 8 and 10). Co-occurrence mostly reflects that stores stock everything daily, not linked buying. Stockout exposure differs sharply: Category 29 runs out on 65% of days (4.1 h) and Category 28 averages 4.4 stockout hours.

RQ4 (redistribution): On the held-out week the profile estimate was within ±3% of true hidden sales, while a flat 50% correction understated them by 23–78%. Over 90 days, stockouts hid an estimated 1.30 million units, about 29% of observed sales. With unsold stock at fully stocked stores at 10% of their sales and transfers on the shelf by 14:00, the LP recovers 148,122 units (11.4%).

8. Managerial Implications

  • Replenish before the peaks: Sales peak at 09:00 and 15:00–16:00 while stockouts build toward closing, so replenishment should land before 08:30 and 14:30, and forecasts should use in-stock demand rather than raw sales.
  • Back promotions with stock: With ε ≈ −1.2, discounts move volume, but discounting stockout-prone items (Categories 29, 28) wastes margin. Gate promotions on safety stock and scale up before weekends (+0.15) and holidays (+0.12).
  • Transfer where stockouts are uneven, order more where they are not: Same-day transfers recover 11–24% of lost demand depending on leftover stock, most in dense cities. Where a category runs out across most stores (21, 28), transfers cannot help and base orders should rise. A pilot in City 0 should first log end-of-day unsold stock for two weeks, then run transfers on alternate days and compare fill rates.

Limitations: Results are observational. Transfer figures are scenario estimates of recoverable demand, an upper bound because the replay knows which stores run out; they are not measured waste reduction, since the data has no inventory or spoilage.

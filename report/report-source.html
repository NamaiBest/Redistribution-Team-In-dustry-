Demand Censoring, Promotional Elasticity, and Same-Day Store Redistribution in Perishable Retail

Course Project Summary Report  |  Business Analytics & Industry 4.0  |  IIT Madras B.S. Degree Programme

1. Problem Statement

Perishable fresh-retail operations experience pronounced intraday shopping waves, promotional discount surges, and calendar or weather shifts, yet frequent daytime out-of-stock events censor observed daily sales below true customer demand. Relying on unadjusted point-of-sale records systematically underestimates demand for high-turnover items, misattributes promotional lift, and misallocates scarce stock. This study investigates the FreshRetailNet-50K dataset ([1]) to quantify latent demand censoring, estimate discount elasticity and environmental demand drivers, benchmark store-group efficiency, and design a same-day store-to-store redistribution model that recovers part of the censored demand.

2. Motivation for Problem Selection

Fresh produce and perishable consumer goods operate under tight shelf-life windows where over-ordering causes spoilage and under-ordering creates immediate lost sales. Both often happen in one city on the same day. Bridging empirical demand modeling from Business Analytics (regression, elasticity, chi-square testing, and Data Envelopment Analysis) with Industry 4.0 inventory and optimization frameworks (Apriori co-movement, stochastic inventory principles, and Linear Programming) enables redistribution decisions grounded in uncensored demand rather than distorted historical sales.

3. Research Questions

  • RQ1 (Intraday Dynamics & Censoring): How do hourly sales and stockout probabilities evolve across the 24-hour trading day, and how severely do daytime stockouts censor observed daily sales within baseline demand tiers?
  • RQ2 (Promotions & Price Elasticity): How do price discounts and promotional campaign flags affect daily sales, and how do linear versus constant-elasticity demand curves and stockouts shape this response?
  • RQ3 (External Drivers & Operational Efficiency): How do calendar events (holidays, weekends) and weather covariates shift demand, and how do management groups compare in relative operational efficiency?
  • RQ4 (Co-Movement & Allocation Policy): Which product categories co-occur across store-days, and how much lost demand can a stockout-aware linear program recover by moving stock between stores?

4. Hypotheses, Objectives, and Constraints

Hypotheses: (H1) Demand exhibits bimodal intraday peaks while cumulative stockouts suppress daily sales once daytime stockout duration grows; (H2) Demand is elastic (ε < −1) under a constant-elasticity curve, with discount depth rather than campaign tags driving lift; (H3) Weekends and holidays increase demand while operating groups vary significantly in efficiency; (H4) Anchor categories exhibit strong store-day co-occurrence, and unsold stock at some stores can recover demand lost at others.

Objectives: Decompose 24-hour sales and stockout arrays to measure censoring gaps; estimate linear and log-log elasticity regressions with multicollinearity checks; evaluate calendar/weather effects via Multiple Linear Regression (MLR) and rank management groups via input-oriented CCR Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA); mine Apriori category rules; and plan same-day transfers between stores in a city with a Linear Program built on validated lost-demand estimates.

Constraints & Scope: Daily and hourly sales in FreshRetailNet-50K are globally normalised volume metrics without unit currency prices, spoilage weights, or physical on-hand inventory levels; discount is a relative price multiplier (1.00 = full price); basket receipts are unobserved, requiring store-day category co-occurrence analysis; and store locations and leftover stock are not recorded, so leftover stock and transfer timing are treated as scenario inputs.

5. Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA)

Analysis was performed on a deterministic 20% hash sample comprising 901,800 store-product-day observations across 19 columns with zero missing values; the redistribution model uses all 4.5 million training rows (18 cities, 898 stores, 865 products, 28 Mar – 25 Jun 2024) and the 7-day evaluation week. Univariate profiling confirmed right-skewed daily sales (overall mean ~0.99, median ~0.60–0.70) and substantial daytime stockout exposure (mean ~3.18 hours out of 16 daytime hours from 06:00 to 22:00). Promotional campaigns (activity_flag = 1) account for 38.02% of observations (342,853 rows) versus 61.98% non-campaign days (558,947 rows). Discount tiers span full price (1.00; 48.09% of rows), light (0.86–0.99; 26.77%), moderate (0.71–0.85; 18.09%), and deep discounts (≤ 0.70; 7.05%). Structural cross-tabulations revealed that 97.79% of active campaign days carry price discounts (mean multiplier 0.8000 versus 0.9783 on non-campaign days), motivating explicit multicollinearity testing in RQ2.

6. Methodology

All analytical techniques strictly align with the Business Analytics and Industry 4.0 syllabus ([2], [3]):

  1. Intraday & Censoring Decomposition (RQ1): Stacked 24-element hours_sale and binary hours_stock_status arrays into 901,800 × 24 matrices to compare overall observed hourly sales against in-stock conditional sales. Series were stratified into four baseline demand quartiles (Q1–Q4) using zero-stockout median sales to evaluate daily sales decay across stockout duration buckets (0h, 1–3h, 4–8h, 9–15h, 16h).
  2. Demand Response & Elasticity Modeling (RQ2): Estimated Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) linear demand models (including discount × activity_flag and discount × stock_hour6_22_cnt interactions) alongside a constant-elasticity log-log specification, ln(sale_amount) = β0 + ε ln(discount) + β2(activity_flag) + β3(stock_hour6_22_cnt), verified via Variance Inflation Factors (VIF).
  3. Calendar/Weather Regression & DEA Benchmarking (RQ3): Conducted a χ² test of independence (with Cramér's V) between holiday_flag and stockout incidence, fitted an extended MLR incorporating weekend, holiday, precipitation, temperature, humidity, and wind predictors, and solved an input-oriented CCR-DEA linear program across management_group_id units (output: mean sales; inputs: stockout burden and discount/precipitation exposure).
  4. Apriori Co-Movement (RQ4): Mined frequent 1- and 2-itemsets across 79,342 active store-days (minimum support 0.05) for the top eight categories.
  5. Lost-Demand Estimation & Redistribution LP (RQ4, final solution): Learned an hourly demand profile (06:00–21:59) per store-product from in-stock hours only, and applied it to stockout hours, scaled by the day's stocked-hour sales. Validated chronologically by hiding hours on fully stocked days of the held-out week. For each city, product and day, a Linear Program (scipy.optimize.linprog) chooses units xij sent from store i to store j to maximise Σ(pj − c)·xij, where pj is the receiver's price multiplier and c the handling cost (0.15 of full price), subject to units sent ≤ the sender's unsold stock and units received ≤ the receiver's lost demand after the transfer arrives. Only stores with no stockout can send; the baseline is no transfers.

7. Results

RQ1 (Diurnal Curve & Censoring): Observed hourly sales peak in the morning at 09:00 (mean 0.1054) with a secondary afternoon wave at 15:00–16:00, whereas hourly stockout rates climb monotonically to 41.94% at 22:00 (daytime average 19.89%). Comparing in-stock conditional sales against observed sales exposes up to a 34.84% daytime hourly censoring gap. Within high-demand tier Q4, mean daily sales fall from 2.9370 (1–3h brief stockouts) and 2.2032 (0h) down to 2.3142 (4–8h), 1.4821 (9–15h), and 0.0768 (16h full-day stockout).

RQ2 (Promotional Elasticity): Deep discounts (≤ 0.70) increase mean daily sales nearly 2.7× over full price (2.1758 vs. 0.8083). The constant-elasticity model outperforms the linear specification (R² = 0.0453 vs. 0.0402) and yields a statistically significant discount elasticity of ε = −1.2555 (p < 0.0001), indicating that a 1% reduction in the price multiplier is associated with a ~1.26% volume expansion. All predictors pass multicollinearity checks (VIF: discount = 2.440, activity_flag = 2.443, stock_hour6_22_cnt = 1.002). Once discount depth is controlled for, activity_flag shows a −8.16% residual shift, confirming that actual price cuts—not campaign tags—drive volume, while the positive discount × stockout interaction (+0.0616) shows stockouts dampen promotional lift.

RQ3 (External Drivers & Unit Efficiency): While the χ² test between holiday_flag and stockout presence is statistically significant due to large sample size (χ² = 5.95, p = 0.0147), Cramér's V = 0.0026 indicates negligible practical dependence. Extending MLR with calendar and weather variables raises R² to 0.2493: weekends (+0.1753, t = 34.58) and holidays (+0.1059, t = 21.93) significantly lift sales, whereas daytime stockouts (−0.0337/hr) and wind level (−0.0666) depress sales. CCR-DEA places management groups 2 and 6 on the efficiency frontier (θ = 1.0000), followed by group 3 (0.9039), group 0 (0.7629), group 5 (0.6545), group 4 (0.5957), and group 1 (0.5792).

RQ4 (Category Co-Movement): Apriori mining identifies Category 8 as a core traffic anchor co-occurring with Categories 21 (lift = 1.2996, support = 0.2074), 10 (lift = 1.2934), 18 (lift = 1.2907), 20 (lift = 1.2213, confidence 8→20 = 78.10%), and 4 (lift = 1.1728, confidence 8→4 = 94.11%).

RQ4 (Lost demand & redistribution, final solution): On the held-out week, the profile estimate was within ±3% of true hidden sales; a flat 50% correction understated them by 23–78%. Over the 90 training days, stockouts hid an estimated 1.30 million units of demand, about 29% of observed sales. With unsold stock at fully stocked stores set to 10% of their sales and transfers on the shelf by 14:00, the LP recovers 148,122 units, or 11.4% of lost demand.

8. Managerial Implications

  • Pre-Peak Replenishment & Censoring Correction: Because sales peak at 09:00 and 15:00–16:00 while stockouts compound toward 22:00, replenishment cycles must occur prior to 08:30 and 14:30, and forecasting pipelines must replace raw historical sales with stockout-adjusted demand estimates.
  • Stock-Backed Promotional Pricing: With elastic demand (ε = −1.26), targeted price discounts drive substantial volume growth, but running discounts on stockout-prone SKUs erodes margin without fulfilling demand. Promotions should be gated on minimum safety-stock coverage and scaled up ahead of weekends (+0.175) and holidays (+0.106).
  • Same-Day Redistribution, Starting in the Densest City: Transfers recover 11–24% of lost demand depending on leftover stock. A pilot should log end-of-day unsold stock per store for two weeks in City 0, then run transfers on alternate days and compare fill rates. Groups 1, 4 and 5 (θ < 0.66) should benchmark against frontier groups 2 and 6, and anchor Category 8 should keep a minimum shelf presence.

Limitations: The replay knows in hindsight which stores run out, so results are an upper bound; all stores in a city are treated as equally reachable.
